The 7.5 g and 10 g flask settings are my estimates: steeps about 0.6× and 0.5× the 5 g times, and a first cup with only about 10–15% more caffeine, because the shorter steep offsets most of the extra leaf. At 1 min or less, count the pour as part of the steep. The box also puts one 5 g piece in a 250 ml teapot, which is 1 g per 50 ml. That matches the Chayu ratio for white, green and scented tea, but it's lighter than the sources' ratios for black and oolong, and than the usual pu-erh ratio. For those, I'd weigh out 8–12 g and shorten each steep; that's my suggestion, not a source.

Two-flask routine

  1. Brew in the titanium flask. Wash if the chart says to, then steep for the 1st time on the chart.
  2. Pour through the strainer into the second 500 ml flask. Off the leaves, the tea stops steeping, so it won't get more bitter.
  3. Drink from the second flask by the cup. Expect a bit less than 500 ml, since the wet leaves keep some back.
  4. Refill the titanium flask at break if you have hot water there. Add the "Later steeps" time to your last steep, plus a minute if the water is cooler than the chart.
  5. Leftover wet leaves: fridge in a covered cup, use within 24 hours, wash again before steeping. Worth it mainly for pu-erh. (General food-handling practice, not from the tea sources.)

Brewing by feel

The chart is for measuring. These principles are for when you stop. They're my synthesis; where one rests on a source, it says so.

1. Pick the mode first

Gongfu takes a little from a lot of leaf each pour, over 6–10 small cups. Long steeps take most of what a brew can give from a little leaf, over 2–4 big cups. The Jesse's box brews 1 g per 50 ml for 15 s in the teapot; the national tasting method uses the same ratio for 5 min. Same leaf, twenty times the time: the mode sets the time, not just the ratio. Don't slide between them. In a flask, 30–60 s steeps are where the pour becomes part of the steep.

2. Long steeps: leaf × time stays about constant

Twice the leaf needs a bit under half the time, because leaves give up flavor fastest at the start. Half the leaf needs roughly twice the time. To save leaf, use less and steep longer. For the 500 ml flask, stay between 5 g (longest, most economical) and 10 g (shorter, for sharing).

3. Gongfu: time barely follows the vessel

Doubling the water from the 120 ml gaiwan to the 250 ml teapot adds only 5 s on the Jesse's box (10 s to 15 s). Short steeps are set by how fast the leaf releases flavor, not by the volume.

4. Leaf needs room

Wet whole-leaf white tea is bulky: 5 g often fills a 120 ml gaiwan by the later steeps (general gongfu experience). Extra leaf takes space, holds water back, slows the pour and brews unevenly when packed. Pressed leaf opens over the 2nd and 3rd steeps, so crowding shows up late. That's why the flask stops at 10 g.

5. Change one knob at a time

Thin: steep longer, go hotter (as the Jesse's box says) or add leaf; to save leaf, go longer first. Bitter or drying: go shorter or about 10°F cooler rather than cutting the leaf (general practice).

6. Calibrate, then stop measuring

Use a 5 g cake as your size reference when breaking the bar. Before a steep is up, pour a splash into a white cup; remember the color of steeps you liked, and match the color rather than the clock. Once, measure what each brew yields at 5 g and at 10 g in your own flask.

How much leaf

The leaf-to-water ratio sets the steep time, not the vessel. A lot of leaf for the water means steeps of seconds; a little means minutes. The same 5 g piece is about 1 g per 24 ml in the 120 ml gaiwan and 1 g per 100 ml in the 500 ml flask. For long steeps I use 1–2 g per 100 ml for every type: 1 g, the caffeine study's ratio rather than a brewing source's rule, for the longest and most economical steeps, and up to 2 g for shorter ones. More than that crowds the vessel (see Brewing by feel), so the calculator shows 5–10 g for every tea at 500 ml. Short steeps use about 2–5 times more leaf, and the amount does depend on type.

China's national tasting method

GB/T 23776-2018 sets how professional tasters brew tea to judge its quality. It uses boiling water for every type and, for most types, twice the flask's leaf, so it's deliberately strong and not a drinking recipe. It's useful for two things: how steep times compare across types, and how much longer pressed tea needs than loose.

Rules that hold across types

My synthesis of the sources below. Where a rule comes from one source, it says so.

Heat follows processing

Tender, unoxidized leaf (green, yellow, fresh white) wants cooler water. Oxidized, fermented, aged or tightly pressed leaf (black, oolong, pu-erh, aged white) takes water at or near a boil.

Leaf and time trade off

More leaf means shorter steeps. For short steeps the sources run from 1 g per 50 ml (green, white, scented) to 1 g per 18–30 ml (aged white, oolong, black). The black tea standard goes up to 15 g in 100 ml for a strong pot. The national tasting method uses 1 g per 50 ml for 4–6 min, which is strong on purpose. The flask's 7.5 g and 10 g settings apply this; see Brewing by feel. Taste the first brew and adjust.

How to lengthen steeps

Short steeps: the sources hold the time or add about 5 s for the first few steeps, then jump by 10–30 s once the tea thins. Aged white reaches 60–120 s late on. Long steeps: add 1–2 min per brew, and 2–3 min late in a ripe pu-erh session.

Wash pressed tea

The Jesse's box washes its pressed white for 3 s, so the chart washes all three whites. The aged white standard makes a quick wet-and-rest optional. The black tea standard skims foam off the first steep instead of washing, and so does the national method's oolong gaiwan method. None of the sources wash green tea. The 10 s pu-erh wash is general practice, not from these sources.

Caffeine rises with time

In the 2025 study, longer steeps extracted significantly more caffeine; water temperature made a significant difference only for oolong. In a 2026 study of three 2-min green tea brews, the second brew had the most caffeine. An earlier study it cites found caffeine roughly halving with each brew.
