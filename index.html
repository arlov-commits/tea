How a source is chosen, in order: the tea's own package; then a source for that specific tea; then a source for its type that brews closest to the vessel's leaf-to-water ratio (the flask is 1:100, the gaiwan 1:24, the teapot 1:50). A source more than 1.5 times off that ratio isn't used. Where no source fits, the value is Claude-imputed and shown in the imputed style; the "Imputed" line below gives the method. Strain every flask brew: tea kept hot on its leaves turns more bitter (Kexin 2018). Caffeine: every first flask brew here works out at about 40–50 mg (derived from the caffeine study; working below), except where a tea says otherwise under its name.

There is no 7.5 or 10 g flask setting any more. No source gives long-steep times for more leaf; the two that use more (ISO 3103 and GB/T 23776, both 1 g per 50 ml) steep longer, 5–6 min, because they are tasting methods.

Brewing principles

What the sources on this page agree on, each with its source. The last one is a suggestion, marked as such.

1. Pick the mode first

The same tea is brewed two ways. Curious Tea gives its moonlight white cake 2.5 g in 250 ml for 3–4 min, or 5 g per 100 ml for 20 s in a gaiwan. Jesse's box brews 1 g per 50 ml for 15 s in a teapot; ISO 3103, at the same 1 g per 50 ml, brews black tea for 6 min. The mode sets the time, not the ratio alone.

2. Long steeps use about 1 g per 100 ml

Curious Tea's white cakes: 2.5 g in 250 ml. Saratoga Tea & Honey: 5 g to 16 oz (1.06 g per 100 ml) for green, white, scented white and pu-erh, and 5 g to 12–16 oz for black. The caffeine study brewed 2 g in 200 ml. Gongfu uses 2–6 g per 100 ml: 1:50 to 1:30 in the Chayu guide, 3–5 g at Curious Tea, 6 g for pu-erh at Yunnan Sourcing.

3. Lengthen the later steeps

Gongfu: the Chayu guide adds 5–20 s between steeps, often holding the same time for two to four steeps first. Long steeps: Saratoga adds 15–30 s a steep for pu-erh and steeps black tea "for increasing intervals", 2–4 more times.

4. Heat follows the type

From the tables here: green 165–212°F, white 185–212°F, black 203–212°F, pu-erh 190–212°F. The long-steep sources run cooler than the gongfu ones for green and raw pu-erh (derived by comparing the sources on this page).

5. Strain in the flask

An insulated flask keeps tea hot on its leaves, which is a much longer hot steep and brings out more bitterness (Kexin 2018). The same piece rejects the claim that it harms health. Its fixes: less leaf, a different tea, or brew first and then fill the flask.

6. Fix thin or bitter tea

Thin: steep longer or go hotter (Jesse's box). Bitter from a long hot steep: less leaf, or brew first and pour into the flask (Kexin 2018).

7. Caffeine rises with time

Longer steeps gave significantly more caffeine; water temperature made a significant difference only for oolong (Ayakdaş 2025). One steep doesn't extract it all. In three 2-min green-tea infusions, the second had the most caffeine (Zhang 2026); an earlier study it cites found caffeine halving with each infusion at 3 min.

8. Calibrate, then measure less

Suggestion, not from a source: use a 5 g piece as your size reference when breaking a bar, and note the colour of steeps you liked in a white cup.

China's national tasting method

GB/T 23776-2018, 茶叶感官审评方法 (Methodology for sensory evaluation of tea), sets how tasters brew tea to judge its quality. Like ISO 3103, it is a test method rather than a drinking recipe. It uses boiling water for every type and, for most, 1 g per 50 ml, twice the flask's leaf. It shows how steep times compare across types, and how much longer pressed tea is steeped than loose.
