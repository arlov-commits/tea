BOX is from the Jesse's Teahouse 月光白 (yuèguāng bái) box. Where a BOX value sits in a merged cell, it applies to the whole white group. Everything else comes from the sources below or is general practice. The box's 85°C is the cool end: the Chayu guide uses 95°C for white and scented tea. Gaiwan times for white, black and pu-erh follow the Chayu guide; all flask and teapot times and brew counts are my synthesis. Black tea gets no wash, following the black tea standard; the 10 s pu-erh wash is common habit, not from these sources. Caffeine is estimated for 5 g in 500 ml, first brew only; a 6 g piece should give roughly a fifth more. The box also puts one 5 g piece in a 250 ml teapot, which is 1 g per 50 ml. That matches the Chayu ratio for white, green and scented tea, but it's lighter than the sources' ratios for black and oolong, and than the usual pu-erh ratio. For those, I'd weigh out 8–12 g and shorten each steep; that's my suggestion, not a source.

Two-flask routine

  1. Brew in the titanium flask. Wash if the chart says to, then steep for the 1st time on the chart.
  2. Pour through the strainer into the second 500 ml flask. Off the leaves, the tea stops steeping, so it won't get more bitter.
  3. Drink from the second flask by the cup. Expect a bit less than 500 ml, since the wet leaves keep some back.
  4. Refill the titanium flask at break if you have hot water there. Add the "Later steeps" time to your last steep, plus a minute if the water is cooler than the chart.
  5. Leftover wet leaves: fridge in a covered cup, use within 24 hours, wash again before steeping. Worth it mainly for pu-erh. (General food-handling practice, not from the tea sources.)

How much leaf

The leaf-to-water ratio sets the steep time, not the vessel. A lot of leaf for the water means steeps of seconds; a little means minutes. The same 5 g piece is about 1 g per 24 ml in the 120 ml gaiwan and 1 g per 100 ml in the 500 ml flask. For long steeps I use 1 g per 100 ml for every type; that's the caffeine study's ratio, not a brewing source's rule, so the calculator shows 5 g for every tea at 500 ml. Short steeps use about 2–5 times more leaf, and the amount does depend on type.

China's national tasting method

GB/T 23776-2018 sets how professional tasters brew tea to judge its quality. It uses boiling water for every type and, for most types, twice the flask's leaf, so it's deliberately strong and not a drinking recipe. It's useful for two things: how steep times compare across types, and how much longer pressed tea needs than loose.

Rules that hold across types

My synthesis of the sources below. Where a rule comes from one source, it says so.

Heat follows processing

Tender, unoxidized leaf (green, yellow, fresh white) wants cooler water. Oxidized, fermented, aged or tightly pressed leaf (black, oolong, pu-erh, aged white) takes water at or near a boil.

Leaf and time trade off

More leaf means shorter steeps. For short steeps the sources run from 1 g per 50 ml (green, white, scented) to 1 g per 18–30 ml (aged white, oolong, black). The black tea standard goes up to 15 g in 100 ml for a strong pot. The national tasting method uses 1 g per 50 ml for 4–6 min, which is strong on purpose. 10 g in the flask would be 1 g per 50 ml; I'd expect it to need roughly half the chart times, but that's my inference. Taste the first brew and adjust.

How to lengthen steeps

Short steeps: the sources hold the time or add about 5 s for the first few steeps, then jump by 10–30 s once the tea thins. Aged white reaches 60–120 s late on. Long steeps: add 1–2 min per brew, and 2–3 min late in a ripe pu-erh session.

Wash pressed tea

The Jesse's box washes its pressed white for 3 s, so the chart washes all three whites. The aged white standard makes a quick wet-and-rest optional. The black tea standard skims foam off the first steep instead of washing, and so does the national method's oolong gaiwan method. None of the sources wash green tea. The 10 s pu-erh wash is general practice, not from these sources.

Fix thin or bitter tea

Thin: steep longer or go hotter, as the Jesse's box says. Bitter or drying: go about 10°F cooler or shorter (general practice).

Caffeine rises with time

In the 2025 study, longer steeps extracted significantly more caffeine; water temperature made a significant difference only for oolong. In a 2026 study of three 2-min green tea brews, the second brew had the most caffeine. An earlier study it cites found caffeine roughly halving with each brew.
